**01 — The Lighthouse Vantage**

Most waterfront homes on the Space Coast are single-story — the view exists,
but it's the only view, from the only level. 940 Whitmire climbs. Three
stories, with a primary suite balcony on the second level and a loft balcony
on the third that takes in the entire harbor. Get off the boat at the dock,
walk up two flights, and you're standing somewhere that feels like a private
observation deck over the river.

the short version of how the levels relate:

## Who's Actually Looking for a Home Like This

The Space Coast's strongest buyer pool right now is relocators — people
moving for work with L3Harris, Northrop Grumman, or Health First, often
coming from denser, colder, or more expensive markets.

For a lot of these buyers, the thing they're searching for is water they can
actually use year-round, not for a few months a year. A home with this
footprint and water access would be right at home on the Chesapeake or in
coastal New England — except there, the view earns its keep seasonally. Here,
it's a year-round asset, and that's a meaningful difference to someone who's
spent winters looking at a frozen dock.

The built-in office nook speaks directly to hybrid and remote professionals —
a workday with a water view out the window isn't a minor detail to this
buyer, it's often the deciding factor. And the flexible lower level reads
differently to someone relocating long-distance: it's room for visiting
family, or a separated home office, or both at different points in life.

## A Word on Insurance — Said Plainly, Up Front

Because this is a waterfront property, flood insurance is part of the real
conversation, and it's one we'd rather hand over early than have a buyer
discover partway through a deal.

The honest version: insurance costs on a property like this are a known, real
number — not a mystery, and not something to be nervous about. A simple
survey called an elevation certificate (typically a few hundred dollars)
documents exactly how the home sits relative to flood risk, and often brings
the insurance number down by doing so. It's the kind of homework that costs
very little and removes a question before anyone has to ask it.

It's also worth knowing this property carries **no HOA** — so where a buyer
coming from a community with monthly association dues budgets for that line
item, this property's buyer budgets for insurance instead. Same kind of
expense, different name, and one less monthly bill of a different kind.

One more honest note: Florida property taxes reset to reflect the sale price
for a new owner, following the same reassessment process every property in
the state goes through. The current tax bill reflects two decades of
homestead-capped growth — a new owner's bill will be different, and that's
true of literally every home sale in Florida, not a quirk of this one.
