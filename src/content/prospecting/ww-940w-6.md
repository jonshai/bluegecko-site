## The Same Shape, at the State Level

This isn't unique to one listing. Florida home values as a whole have been
normalizing after the 2021–2024 run-up — moving back toward the long-run
trend line, not away from it.

{{gallery:2}}

The 2024 peak (around $401K statewide) was the anomaly relative to the
long-run 3.1% trend from 2000. The pullback since then — to roughly $377K — is
the market returning toward where it was always headed, not collapsing away
from where it was.

Put the two charts side by side and they tell the same story at different
scales: a market correcting toward a long-run number, most of the way through
that correction already. The honest question isn't whether that correction
continues — it's whether it makes more sense to finish crossing that distance
now, from close to the destination, or to start the same distance over again
from a higher number next season. We're not going to answer that one for you.
The data's right here either way.

## The Complete Disclosure Picture

Everything that follows is the kind of thing a buyer's agent would otherwise
spend the inspection period digging up. Here, it's just already in the
folder.

**Flood insurance** — An elevation certificate ($400–$900, typically a few
days' turnaround) documents exactly how this home sits relative to base flood
elevation, and frequently reduces the insurance premium by 15–30% per foot of
elevation above that line. Paired with an actual insurance quote, this turns
"what will insurance cost" from a guess into a number — before a buyer ever
has to ask.

**No HOA** — Confirmed. No monthly association dues, no association
restrictions, no special assessments. For a buyer comparing this property
against an HOA community, the insurance line item above effectively takes the
place of the dues line item — same budget category, different name.

**Property taxes** — The current bill reflects a homestead exemption built up
over more than two decades of ownership. Florida law reassesses property tax
based on sale price for a new owner — true of every property in the state,
not specific to this one. A buyer's agent will run this number regardless;
having it stated plainly up front means there's nothing to "discover" later.

**Why this matters more than it might seem:** most negotiating leverage during
escrow comes from things one side didn't expect. A complete disclosure
package — survey, elevation certificate, insurance quote, tax context — means
there's very little left to discover, which tends to mean very little left to
negotiate over. If a number comes in different than a buyer expected, the
conversation isn't "what concession should this cost you" — it's "here's the
number we already gave you, and here's who to call if you want to see if it
can come down." That's not a defensive posture. It's just what happens when
the homework is already done.
