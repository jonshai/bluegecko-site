## The brag book

Every seller has things they'd love to tell a buyer. The way the light comes in around six. The sandhill cranes that show up in the backyard some mornings. Why this spot on the canal is the quiet one. None of that belongs in a listing, and none of it works if someone's following a buyer from room to room saying it out loud — that's the fastest way to make a person feel sold to instead of shown around.

A brag book solves this. One folder, sitting on the counter, that says everything worth saying, in the seller's own words, without anyone having to say it out loud. The buyer reads it when they're ready, on their own time.

The personal details are the heart of it — but alongside them, the practical stuff builds real confidence too: disclosures, a cost-of-ownership estimate, a pre-listing inspection if one's been done, wind mitigation paperwork (which can meaningfully lower a buyer's insurance quote), a survey, flood zone documentation, service records on the big systems, any transferable warranties. A buyer who sees the 2023 renovation documented — not just described — starts the conversation from confidence instead of suspicion. A lender reviewing the file sees the same thing: a well-supported, well-documented property, which tends to make the financing side of a transaction smoother for everyone.

For this house specifically, the renovation list already gives the book a strong foundation: new plumbing throughout (no cast iron anywhere — the thing island buyers specifically worry about), impact windows and doors, a 2019 roof, gas water heater and gas heat, alongside the kitchen and finishes a buyer sees the moment they walk in.

## Who this house is for

Three listings reached a lot of people — the photos were good, the listings went where listings go. What's harder is knowing, specifically, who's going to fall for a house like this, and making sure the story reaches them. "Any buyer" and "the buyer" aren't the same search. We think there are two buyers, specifically, who'd see this house the way it deserves to be seen.

**The HOA refugee.** Someone who's spent years paying a monthly fee for a community pool they used twice a summer, a gate that's broken half the time, and a committee that once sent a letter about the color of their door. They're done — not angry, just done. This house has no HOA and no CDD fee. What that buyer gets is the backyard above, entirely theirs, with zero meetings about any of it.

Here's the part that makes it click: that buyer has been paying HOA dues for years without really thinking about it anymore — it's just part of the monthly number. Living on the water here comes with its own monthly number too, insurance chief among it. For this buyer, that's not a new expense to absorb. It's the same line item, doing something different. The fee that used to buy a gate and a clubhouse now buys a house on the water with a pool, a canal, and no one to ask permission. Same habit, better outcome.

**The relocator who's done the math already.** The Space Coast pulls in people from aerospace, defense, and tech — L3Harris, Northrop Grumman, and the broader contractor pool around them. A lot of those folks are moving from places where this house's price tag wouldn't get them a two-bedroom condo, let alone a pool, a canal, and an island. For that buyer, a fully renovated home — new plumbing, impact windows, updated systems, all done — isn't a luxury. It's a relief. They're not used to homes where everything's already been handled. When it is, they notice.

For both of these buyers, the island lifestyle and the renovation aren't separate pitches — they're one pitch told from two directions: here's the world you're moving into, and here's the part of it that's entirely yours, with nothing left to fix and no one to ask permission.
