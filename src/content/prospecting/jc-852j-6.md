**How automated valuation models work — and what they are actually measuring**

Automated valuation models, or AVMs, are statistical estimates of market value
produced by algorithms that analyze publicly available data. They are not appraisals.
They do not account for condition, upgrades, or features a data feed cannot capture.
What they are is a consistent, real-time signal of where the market places a property
based on what comparable homes have sold for — and more importantly, they are exactly
what buyers are looking at when they evaluate whether your ask is reasonable before
they schedule a showing.

A buyer who pulls up 852 Jordan Ave on Zillow sees the Zestimate before they see the
listing photos. A buyer using Redfin sees the Redfin Estimate the same way. When the
ask substantially exceeds what those tools show, buyers and their agents use the gap
as a negotiating anchor — or they simply move on to a listing where the numbers align.

Different AVM providers use different methodologies, different data feeds, and
different weighting of recent sales versus older comparables. No single model is
definitively correct. What a cluster of models in tight agreement produces is a
consensus — and a tight consensus is more meaningful than any individual estimate.
When six independent models land within $19,000 of each other on a property in the
$400,000–$430,000 range, the market has a clear and consistent view of where the
home sits.

RPR — Realtors Property Resource — $424,640
RPR draws its data directly from MLS transaction records. It is the most
professionally-sourced AVM available because it reflects actual closed sale prices
from the same databases agents use to run comparative market analyses. RPR's estimated
range for this property is $403,400 to $445,900, with a year-over-year change of
+3.26% and a month-over-month change of +$940.

A note on that year-over-year figure: individual property AVM year-over-year changes
are inherently noisy. They reflect which specific comparable sales happened to fall
within the model's radius and time window during a given period. A single nearby sale
at an outlier price can move an individual property's figure significantly without the
market itself having changed. County, state, and national year-over-year figures
average across thousands of transactions and are substantially more reliable as trend
indicators. The +3.26% figure for this property is context, not a forecast.

The buyer-facing number that appears on every Redfin property page. It sits at the
high end of the consensus cluster but is within range — not an outlier for this
property. Redfin weights recent sales heavily and updates frequently. This is what

**The long-run context — where Florida values stand**

Since the late 1800s, real estate values have followed a remarkably consistent
long-run trend of approximately 3.1% annual appreciation. This is not a policy
choice or a coincidence. It reflects the relationship between housing supply,
population growth, construction costs, and the general pace of economic expansion.
Spikes happen. Corrections happen. The market returns to the baseline. In over a
century of data, it has never not returned.

The pandemic period produced one of the most dramatic above-baseline surges in
that history. Florida values peaked well above the 3.1% baseline in 2024 and have
been normalizing downward since. The gap between where values sit today and where
the long-run baseline sits remains substantial — normalization has further to go.

{{gallery:1}}

Coastal Florida tends to follow the national trend with a lag. Other markets soften
first and Florida follows. Local Indian River County values currently sit above the
statewide median — the local gap to the long-run baseline is larger than the
statewide picture suggests.

What would need to happen for this trend to reverse? The market shift in February
was significant enough to change the direction of travel. Reversing that direction
requires something equally significant pushing the other way — a sustained drop in
mortgage rates, a broad expansion of buyer purchasing power, a policy shift large
enough to move the needle in a lasting way. None of those conditions are currently
in the forecast. We plan on what the data shows today, with the honest
acknowledgment that no one has a crystal ball. Waiting for a reversal that is not
in the data is a strategy built on hope rather than evidence.

**The DOM problem — why it compounds**

Days on Market is not a neutral statistic. Think about a clearance rack at a retail
store. An item on clearance is not necessarily defective — but it has been marked
down, and every shopper who passes it asks the same quiet question: if this was
worth full price, why didn't someone buy it? Mark it down again and that question
gets louder. A third markdown and the item is not just discounted, it is suspect.
The buyer who eventually picks it up needs the price to be an exceptional value
just to overcome the doubt the markdown history created.

A listing with a long DOM history works exactly the same way. When a buyer sees
that a home has been on the market for six months without selling, the reaction
is not a market analysis. It is a gut read: it's been for sale this long and not
sold yet — something must be wrong with it. That quiet suspicion shapes showing
requests, offer amounts, and inspection negotiations in ways that do not favor
the seller.

The buyer who eventually engages with a high-DOM listing is not the owner-occupant
who fell in love with the screened lanai and the floor package. It is the investor
whose entire strategy depends on the seller being ready to accept less than market
value to end the ordeal. That is the clearance rack buyer — and getting there is
avoidable with the right entry price.

Cumulative Days on Market (CDOM) follows a listing into its next iteration.
A relist resets the active listing date and creates a new-listing flag in search
results. That is valuable. It works best when the price is right enough that the
listing goes under contract before anyone has time to go looking for the history.
