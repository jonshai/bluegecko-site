## Why "lower the price" didn't speed things up

Here's the mechanism, for anyone curious about it. When a listing goes live, it gets a short window of outsized attention — buyer-agent search alerts, portal "new listing" sorting, the first wave of interested eyes. That window is real, but it's also a one-time resource. Every time a listing gets relisted, it draws on that resource again — but the *cumulative* days-on-market history doesn't reset. It's right there in the public record, visible to any buyer's agent who looks (the same record we pulled to write this page, in about ten minutes, using nothing but a public address search).

A property's cumulative history matters because pricing above what the data supports doesn't usually get a listing rejected outright — it gets it *deprioritized*. Buyers searching with a price ceiling simply won't see a listing above that ceiling in their results. Buyers who do see it tend to file it as "the one we'll circle back to" rather than "the one we're choosing between right now" — and "circle back to" rarely converts, no matter how good the house is once someone's actually inside it.

The open house that happened during the second listing is a good data point here: it suggests real traffic existed. Traffic that didn't convert to an offer, on a home this strong, points toward exactly this kind of deprioritization — not toward anything wrong with the house itself.

## The market, in context

The 2023 sale of this home — $710,000 — landed at almost exactly the statewide peak. Florida home values (Zillow's Home Value Index, via the Federal Reserve's FRED database, series FLUCSFRCONDOSMSAMID — statewide data, the most granular long-run series publicly available, though directionally representative of coastal Brevard) peaked around April 2024 and have eased gently since, down roughly 6% from that peak as of early 2026. The 2023 sale wasn't a bad number — it landed right at the top of a real, statewide surge. It's just that the surge has since started to ease, the way surges do.

Here's the longer view, because it matters. A simple 3.1% annual baseline — roughly the long-run pace of US home price growth, in line with general inflation — drawn from Florida's year-2000 values shows something worth knowing: Florida has run *above* that baseline for almost this entire century. There was a sharp surge from 2005 to 2007 (values reached nearly twice the 3.1% baseline), a hard correction from 2008 to 2011 that brought values back down near the baseline by 2012, and then a long climb back up — culminating in the 2021–2024 surge that this home's 2023 sale was part of.

The pattern that emerges: Florida has done this before. A surge, a peak, a gentle multi-year correction back toward (not below) trend, and then growth resumes. The 2008–2011 correction took several years and wasn't a sign anything was wrong with any individual home — it was the market working through a surge. What's happening now, statewide, looks like the early stage of the same kind of correction, just earlier in its arc.

None of this means the 2023 price was a mistake — it was a real transaction at a real peak. It does mean that pricing decisions made by comparing to that 2023 number are, in effect, comparing to the top of a cycle that has historically taken a few years to fully play out. Independent estimates today reflect where the broader market has already moved — which is the $650,000–$680,000 range referenced earlier.

## The full cost-of-ownership picture

A new owner starts year one with no exemption at all, taxed on the full reassessed value. If that new owner makes this their primary residence, they can apply for their own homestead exemption the following year, which brings the number down from there — but year one is year one, for anyone.
